The [Steam Frame]() is a standalone VR headset, designed and manufactured by Valve, starting at 1,049 USD: certainly, a princely sum for the average gamer. Its two primary use cases are streaming VR games from your computer, and playing [pancake games]() of a suitably-dated vintage. Its controllers - a hybrid of traditional VR controllers and a gamepad - are designed to enable this. As a whole, the package is quite good at this, and "a Steam Deck on your face" is not an overstatement.

With that being said: I have had my Frame for a few weeks, and I want you to ignore everything just stated. The controllers are nice, indeed, but they're not how I've been using it. The gameplay is delightful, but I've barely played anything. $1,049 is a lot of money, but it's a comparative bargain. And it may be a VR headset, but that's not what it really is.

No, the Steam Frame is one specific thing to me. One thing I have wanted for a long, long time.

The Steam Frame is an honest-to-god *spatial computer*.

# Apple, Eat Your God Damn Heart Out

In 2023, Apple announced the [Vision Pro](). In 2024, I went to great expense and effort (or rather: my American friend did) to purchase and import a week-one unit into Sweden. I did this, despite all good reason telling me otherwise, because I saw the dream that they were presenting, and it aligned with my own dream: a portable - nay, a _spatial_ - computer; one that I could take with me, work with, watch content in, and immerse myself in - a computer that could finally free me from the tyranny of the desk and monitors.

Two years later, the only immersion my AVP finds is in the drawer in which it serves its banishment. You are likely to know this story, for it is the story of most Vision Pro owners, but I would be remiss to not relate it to you. The Vision Pro is an incredible - and incredibly compromised - preview of a future that doesn't exist yet, and quite frankly, perhaps it shouldn't. It is a future in which Apple own your computer, not you, and the treats they offer you cannot possibly make up for that.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The Steam Frame has objectively worse hardware than the two-year-old Vision Pro. Its processor is worse. Its passthrough is worse. Its displays are worse. Its battery life is worse. It has no way to communicate to people on the outside. Its lenses are - well, no, they're significantly better, but they're targeting much larger display panels, so it's not fair to compare them. The Vision Pro is three times the price, and you can tell.

And yet! And _yet!_ It is not worth three times the price. It is not worth two times the price. If given the option of the Frame or the AVP at the same price, I found not struggle for a moment as to my choice. I would take the Frame, as compromised as it is, because its imperfections belie the truth: that the dream I'm chasing is real, and it can be enjoyed today.

# Slip On, Slip Off

[comparison photo of the Frame and the Vision Pro and the Beyond]

Frame:
- It's so small! I can fold it up and pick it up and chuck it into my bag
- No external battery pack means I can put it on and take it off without accidentally strangling myself
- I have the option to use an external battery pack if I want, which is good, because its battery life is quite poor
- Extraordinarily well-balanced. The most comfortable headset I own. Yes, even more than the Bigscreen Beyond 2e, which is a fraction of its weight and isn't standalone
- The display panels are 2160x2160 LCDs. This is... not great. This has been the standard for several years, and it is limiting for the kind of use I want to engage in; but I can live with it. Now, am I a little frustrated by the firmware leaks indicating the development of [a 4K OLED Steam Frame]()? Of course, but I have the grace to accept the things that cannot be changed, and the courage to change the things that can. And, oh boy, [can you](#the-future-starts-with-you).
- The speakers are acceptable. They're no Vision Pro speakers, or Valve Index BMR speakers. They are convenient, though, and you can always use the USB port to run wired audio if you don't mind a dongle. And never say never, for I [hear rumours of better audio hidden in the mists]()...

Vision Pro:
- all of its weight is centered on the front, and is not well-balanced
- it's unwieldy to carry around, requiring a case
- its spatial controllers are third-party, from another vendor, and offer fewer inputs
- much higher resolution displays, with much better contrast thanks to OLED, but you will not be enjoying them through the optics, which have a tiny sweet spot and are subject to significant glare

# Worse Is Better

- I am genuinely quite impressed by all the stops Apple pulled out for visionOS. They put a great deal of effort into thinking about how computing in XR should work. About how 2D and 3D content should interact. About how content should stay fixed in place for your environment.
- The hand + eye-tracking interface for visionOS is still innovative, even if it does fatigue your eyes. Indeed, it is the first HCI system that has required me to do active _workouts_ for my eyes.
- From a UX perspective, visionOS is strictly better.

But none of that matters, because visionOS is not a useful operating system for productivity. Apple innovated. Apple polished. Apple did everything right by Apple standards. And that's why they fucked it all up, because _you can't do anything on it_. If the Frame is a Steam Deck on your face, the Vision Pro is an iPad on your face, with all that entails.

You will use the software that they allow you to use. You will enjoy Apple's acrimonious relationship with developers, and the resulting lack of apps. You will run into a point of friction with the experience, and you will not be able to do anything about it. visionOS will always be derived from i(Pad)OS, not macOS, and you will have an experience commensurate with that.

- Sure, the software is buggy as hell and the battery life sucks - I've had to restart it a few times already - but it is, unlike its competitors, actually usable
- And, notably, software is fixable

- Real Linux! Real software! Real development!

# But Why?

- This is all fine and good, but why care about spatial computing at all? What's wrong with traditional computing?
- In the limit, spatial computing is a superset of desk computing. A sufficiently good spatial computer will be able to render displays in lieu of your monitors, with the only difference being that other people won't be able to see what you're seeing by default.
- But a superset lets you go beyond. A superset untethers you from your desk. A superset makes your personal computer truly personal.
- You can use your computer in any shape, anywhere, and any posture, without being bound by the tyranny of the rectangle. You don't have to sit behind an enormous monitor to be able to look at everything you want to look at. You don't have to be hunched over to work on the go.
- Your environment can be whatever you want it to be. It doesn't matter if you're in your bedroom, a plane, or a train.
- And yes, as I alluded to before, you will lose the social component by default, but it doesn't have to be that way. Apple have done their preliminary exploration with [EyeSight]() and [app sharing](), and even though these efforts didn't pan out, it doesn't mean that it's impossible; it just needs more time.
- George may look goofy in these photos (sorry, love you!), he is still pioneering a form of computing that I hope lays in front of us.

# The Future Starts With You

- It's extensible, both hardware and software: people are already breaking apart [the software](<https://github.com/lhns/steam-frame-nix>), and it has a hardware extension port that carries real potential, as evidenced by the [Arcturus camera](<https://arcturus.vision/>) and [the firmware page]()
- I'm running system-wide Nix for my software and configuration, and it's all synced up with my other NixOS systems.
- My particular hat in the ring is [lunette](), but there's [Stardust XR](), [Cloud Cafe](), [KDE VR](), [Simula](), [FrameTop](), and others (detail their advantages)

# Conclusion

- This is the realisation of the dream I've been chasing since I bought the Vision Pro, since I was the first to preorder the Simula One, and since I used [Virtual Desktop with the DK2 twelve years ago](). Finally. A. Computer. On. My. Face. 
- So, thank you, Valve. I wish that you'd brought out the [OLED version]() sooner, but you have done what ten years of standalone headsets could not: you have brought me a computer
